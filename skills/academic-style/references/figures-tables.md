# Figures and tables

Readers look at the floats first to decide whether the document is worth reading. **Each one
must stand on its own, and each one must earn its place** by contributing something the
argument needs.

## Table or figure?

- **Table** — precise numerical values; too few numbers to make a decent graph; data compiled
  from several sources or component groups.
- **Figure** — trends and relations between variables; a lot of numbers; a message that must
  land immediately.

If you are unsure, try both and keep the one that communicates. Then **delete the other**: a
data set appears as a figure *or* a table, never both, and never as several re-worked versions
of itself.

## Captions

The caption states **the point of the float, not its topic**. *"Some fatty acids present in
oils"* says nothing; say what the figure shows.

A caption must let a reader extract the information without the main text. Include:

- what is plotted or tabulated, and for which group or period;
- **sample sizes**;
- **what error bars mean** — standard deviation, standard error, or a confidence interval, and
  at what level;
- every symbol, abbreviation, and acronym used in the float;
- the source, for anything not your own (see below).

The caption is always a compromise on length. Whatever will not fit belongs in Materials and
methods, not in a longer caption.

Convention: **table titles go above the table, figure captions below the figure.**

## Referring to floats in the text

Put the cross-reference **at the end of a short descriptive sentence**:

> Sampling was conducted throughout the year (Table 1).

Not:

> Table 1 gives details of sampling.

The first sentence carries a finding; the second is a signpost that costs a sentence and says
nothing. Every float is referred to at least once, and the text never refers to a float that
does not exist.

Number figures and tables in **separate sequences**, each in order of first mention. Do not
interleave the two sequences.

## Table construction

- Parts: title, column headings, row headings, the field, and footnotes.
- **Horizontal rules only** — one above the header, one below it, one at the foot. No vertical
  lines, no full borders. Use white space to show grouping.
- Keep the structure as simple as the message allows.
- Arrange data the way people read: left to right, top to bottom.
- Labels must stay legible after reduction to roughly 60% of the drawn size.
- **A long table belongs in the supplement**, with a summary table or figure in the body.
- Tables belong in Materials and methods too, whenever the reader needs a large block of
  quantitative setup.

## Figure construction

- **Dependent variable on the y axis; independent variable on the x axis.** Where there is a
  real independent variable, reversing the axes is wrong, not just unconventional.
- Keep it simple enough that the message arrives immediately.
- Publication figures differ from talk figures. For a paper or report: two dimensions, not
  three; no gridlines; thin axis lines; grey tones rather than bright colour where the
  publisher asks for it; and **variability shown, with the meaning of the error bars and the
  sample size given in the caption**. A slide can trade information for impact; a figure in a
  report cannot.
- Use one consistent symbol and colour coding across all panels of a multi-panel figure, and
  label panels (A), (B), (C).
- Put a regression equation next to its line rather than in the caption, and report fit as
  either *p* or R² and *n* — whichever keeps the panel readable.
- Check that the figure still reads in greyscale and for colour-vision deficiency.

## A fitted line is a claim

This is the part that is easy to get wrong.

- A **scatter plot with no line** shows the data and invites the reader to interpret. The
  caption says *"Relationship between…"* only once you have asserted one.
- **Drawing a regression line asserts a relationship.** The caption wording should change to
  match — and you should be willing to defend the claim.
- **A single line pooled across groups asserts that the relationship generalises across those
  groups.** That is a stronger claim than a line per group, and it needs support.
- **Which variable goes on x asserts which one is independent.** Where neither is — two
  compositional variables, say — you can show the association but you do not have an
  independent variable, and the wording must not imply one.

## Reusing published figures

- A figure taken from a published source cites that source in the caption, with enough detail
  to reach the primary source.
- **"Adapted from"** when you redrew or altered it; **"Data from"** or **"Data presented by"**
  when you re-plotted the underlying numbers into a graph that never appeared in the original.
- Re-plotting published data to extend an interpretation is legitimate. Presenting it as your
  own is not.
