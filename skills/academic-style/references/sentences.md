# Sentences, voice, and word choice

Write as an analytical scientist addressing specialists: precise, compact, technically
explicit, evidence-led. Cautious about inference, direct about supported findings. Information
density matters more than formality. Do not imitate generic "academic" prose.

## Sentences

- Medium-length declarative sentences — roughly 25–30 words — with occasional short sentences
  as anchors.
- Split any sentence carrying several independent logical turns.
- Avoid stacked subordinate clauses. If a sentence needs three commas to stay upright, it is
  two sentences.
- Prefer specific subjects and strong verbs to nominalisations. *"Catches declined"*, not
  *"a decline in catches was observed"*.

## Paragraphs

One identifiable job per paragraph: **claim or purpose → evidence or method → necessary
qualification → implication**, where an implication belongs. Link paragraphs so the logic
carries; do not make the reader guess the connection.

Remove any concluding sentence that merely repeats the preceding result.

## Voice

Active first-person plural where it is natural — *"we estimated"*, *"we observed"* — and
passive where the procedure or the result matters more than who did it. Both are correct
scientific English.

**A project-specific voice rule overrides this.** Never introduce first-person plural into a
document that deliberately avoids it.

## Transitions

Use them to express logic that is actually there, especially contrast and consequence:
*however, while, despite, consequently, therefore, yet*. Do not add them automatically, and do
not chain them.

## What to cut

- Generic academic filler and throat-clearing.
- Inflated phrasing where a plain word exists.
- Repeated mini-conclusions.
- Unsupported promotional language — *novel, unprecedented, comprehensive* — unless the
  evidence carries it.
- Vague nouns: *factors, aspects, patterns, issues, dynamics*, used without a referent.
- Redundant hedging (hedging that changes the interpretation stays).
- Explanations of standard concepts the audience already has.

## Phrases to delete on sight

- *"For the first time we report…"* — if it is not the first time, this is dual publication;
  if someone else showed it, the work is not news.
- *"To the best of our knowledge, nothing has been published on…"* / *"As far as we are
  aware…"* — did you run an exhaustive search, or not bother?
- *"All protocols were carried out carefully."* / *"The utmost care was taken…"* / *"Extreme
  care was exercised…"* — statements of the obvious, and the last two are exaggerations too.

## Information density: an example

> When I was on the beach I saw three dead fish.

> When I was walking along the shoreline at low tide yesterday morning I found two dead fish
> washed up on to the sandy beach, and saw another floating at the water surface.

The second is longer and better. *Walking along the shoreline* gives a search mode and a
transect; *at low tide* says sea, not lake, and *yesterday morning* makes the tide state
recoverable; *washed up on to* attributes deposition to wave action; *sandy* excludes shingle;
the three fish are given distinct positions. Every added word buys information. **The increase
in information transfer justifies the increase in length.**

Concision means cutting words that buy nothing — not cutting words.

## Do not leave interpretation to the reader

> The results given in Table 1 clearly show that fish fed the different diets grew at
> different rates.

> Fish fed diets B and C grew at similar rates and faster than those given diets A and D,
> whereas fish fed diet E grew slowest (Table 1).

The first is vague and invites the reader to reach a conclusion that may not be yours.

## Grammar policy

Correct these silently while preserving meaning and voice: articles, prepositions,
subject–verb agreement, count and uncountable nouns, word order, parallelism, punctuation,
misplaced modifiers, compound hyphenation, and literal or non-idiomatic constructions.

**Flag instead of fixing** whenever the correction would change a scientific claim, a causal
interpretation, a temporal relation, or a referent. Ambiguity in a scientific sentence is
sometimes the author's uncertainty showing; resolving it silently invents a finding.

## Small conventions

- Treat *data* as plural in formal prose unless the project says otherwise.
- Assume a specialist reader. Define study-specific concepts and abbreviations; do not define
  the field's common vocabulary.
- Define every acronym at first use, and do not introduce one you use only once.
- Keep terminology, symbols, and units identical across prose, equations, tables, captions,
  legends, and code. Drift between them is a real error, not a stylistic one.

## Rhetorical devices to avoid in a research document

Quotations, boxes, rhetorical questions, philosophical or personal conclusions, and metaphor
carrying an argument. They belong in a thesis, an essay, or a talk — not in a paper or a
report. Vivid framing is occasionally right in a title or an opening line; it is never a
substitute for a result.
